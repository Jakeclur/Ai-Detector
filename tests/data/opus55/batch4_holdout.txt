The power went out at 7:14pm, right in the middle of the storm, right in the middle of dinner.

For a second nobody moved. The fridge stopped humming, the TV in the lounge room clicked off, and the only light came from the lightning outside, flashing white across the kitchen every few seconds. Then Mum found the torch in the drawer, Dad found the candles from Grandma's birthday cake, and we finished our spaghetti by the light of three half-melted number sevens.

Our phones were all low. The internet was gone. My little brother Sam asked what we were supposed to do now, as if a world without Wi-Fi was something he'd only read about.

Dad disappeared into the hall cupboard and came back with a pack of cards held together by a rubber band. Half of them were bent. The seven of hearts was missing, so he wrote one on a piece of cardboard.

He taught us a game called Five Hundred, which he said he used to play with his brothers during blackouts on the farm. The rules made no sense at first. Sam cheated constantly. Mum kept forgetting what trumps were. I won two hands in a row and refused to stop talking about it.

The power came back at 10:30. The lights flickered on, the fridge started humming again, and we all looked at each other, a bit disappointed.

Nobody reached for their phone. Dad shuffled the deck. "One more hand," he said.

Ipswich's central library will open until 9pm on weeknights from next month, after a trial showed strong demand from students and shift workers.

The extended hours, up from the current 6pm closing time, will begin on 3 February and apply Monday to Thursday. Weekend hours will not change.

Ipswich City Council ran a twelve-week trial late last year. Library staff recorded more than 6,500 visits during the extra evening hours, with the busiest periods in the weeks before end-of-year exams. A survey of trial users found that 41 per cent were secondary or university students and 23 per cent worked shifts that made daytime visits difficult.

Library services manager Karen Whitlock said many people had told staff they had nowhere quiet to study at home.

"For a lot of families, the library is the only place with reliable internet and a quiet desk," she said. "Being open later means students can come after sport or work, not just straight after school."

The change will cost about $180,000 a year, mostly for additional staff. Council has also approved extra security patrols in the surrounding car park after some trial users raised safety concerns about walking to their cars after dark.

Bookings for study rooms and computers will be available online. The council said it would review the evening hours after twelve months and consider extending them to other branches if usage remained high.

Should Australia rely more on renewable energy?

Australia has some of the best conditions in the world for renewable energy, with abundant sunshine, strong winds and large areas of open land. As the cost of solar and wind has fallen and the effects of climate change have become clearer, there is a strong case for relying more on renewables, although the transition comes with real challenges.

The most important argument is climate change. Burning coal and gas for electricity is one of Australia's largest sources of greenhouse gas emissions. Replacing them with renewable sources would greatly reduce these emissions and help Australia meet its international commitments.

Renewables are also becoming cheaper. Large-scale solar and wind farms now produce electricity at a lower cost than new coal or gas plants. Millions of Australian households have installed rooftop solar, which lowers their power bills and reduces demand on the grid.

However, renewable energy is not always available when it is needed. The sun does not shine at night, and wind can drop for days. To keep the lights on, Australia will need more batteries, pumped hydro and new transmission lines, which are expensive and take years to build.

There are also social costs. Communities in regions such as the Hunter Valley and the Latrobe Valley depend on coal jobs, and they will need support to retrain and find new industries.

Overall, Australia should rely more on renewable energy, but the transition must be planned carefully so that power remains reliable and no communities are left behind.

How do planes fly?

Planes fly because their wings are shaped to create lift, a force that pushes the plane upwards.

An aeroplane wing is curved on top and flatter underneath, and it meets the oncoming air at a slight upward angle. As the plane moves forward, air flows over and under the wing. The shape and angle of the wing push air downwards. By Newton's third law, if the wing pushes air down, the air pushes the wing up.

You can also think about it in terms of pressure. The air moving over the curved top of the wing speeds up, and faster-moving air has lower pressure. The air underneath stays at a higher pressure. The difference in pressure between the bottom and top of the wing creates lift.

For a plane to fly, four forces need to be in balance. Lift pushes it up, and weight pulls it down. Thrust, produced by the engines, pushes it forward, and drag, caused by air resistance, holds it back. When lift is greater than weight, the plane climbs. When thrust is greater than drag, it speeds up.

This is why planes need to go fast to take off. A wing only produces enough lift when air is moving over it quickly. A large passenger jet usually needs to reach around 250 to 290 kilometres per hour before it can leave the ground.

Pilots control the plane by moving flaps on the wings and tail, which change the airflow and let them climb, descend and turn.

What is a recession?

A recession is a period when a country's economy shrinks instead of growing. In Australia, a recession is often defined as two quarters in a row (six months) where the economy's total output, measured by gross domestic product or GDP, goes down.

In a normal year, an economy grows. Businesses sell more, people earn more and new jobs are created. During a recession, that process goes into reverse. People and businesses spend less, so companies make less money. To cut costs, businesses may stop hiring or lay off workers. Those workers then have less money to spend, which hurts other businesses even more.

Recessions can be caused by many things. A sudden shock, like the COVID-19 pandemic, can stop spending almost overnight. High interest rates can make borrowing expensive, so people buy fewer houses and cars. A financial crisis, like the one in 2008, can make banks stop lending.

Governments and central banks try to soften recessions. The Reserve Bank can lower interest rates to encourage borrowing and spending. The government can spend more money directly, cut taxes or give payments to households, as it did with JobKeeper in 2020.

For ordinary people, the main effect of a recession is usually on jobs. Unemployment tends to rise, and it can take years to fall again after the economy recovers.

Australia went almost thirty years without a recession, from 1991 to 2020, which is one of the longest runs of any developed country.

How to deal with stress

Work out what's actually stressing you. Stress can feel like one big cloud, but it usually has specific causes. Write them down. Seeing "maths assignment due Friday, argument with Mum, worried about netball selection" on paper often makes things feel more manageable than a general sense of panic.

Break big tasks into small ones. If an assignment feels overwhelming, don't think about the whole thing. Think about the next step, like finding three sources or writing the introduction. Small wins build momentum.

Move your body. Exercise is one of the most reliable ways to reduce stress. It doesn't have to be intense. A twenty-minute walk, shooting hoops or dancing in your room all help your body burn off stress hormones.

Protect your sleep. Stress makes it harder to sleep, and lack of sleep makes stress worse. Try to keep a regular bedtime and put your phone away at least half an hour before.

Talk to someone. Friends, family, a teacher or the school counsellor can help you see things differently, or simply listen. You don't need a big problem to deserve support.

Be careful with how you cope. Scrolling for hours, skipping meals or staying up late gaming can feel like a break, but they often leave you feeling worse.

Some stress is normal and can even help you perform. But if it's affecting your sleep, eating or mood for weeks, reach out for help. Kids Helpline (1800 55 1800) is free and available any time.

Review: The Little Fig, Paddington

The Little Fig is the kind of café you walk past twice before you notice it. It's tucked between a bookshop and a dry cleaner on Given Terrace, with only six tables inside and a few stools by the window. It's worth finding.

I went on a Saturday morning and waited about fifteen minutes for a table, which seems normal. The staff were friendly and kept the queue updated, and one of them brought out water while we waited.

The menu is short, which I liked. I ordered the ricotta hotcake with poached pear and honeycomb butter. It was thick, soft and not too sweet, and the pear balanced it nicely. My friend had the mushroom toast with whipped feta and a poached egg, which she said was the best she'd had in Brisbane. The portions were generous for the price, around $22 for most dishes.

The coffee was excellent. They use beans from a small roaster in West End, and my flat white was smooth and strong without being bitter.

The only downsides were the noise and the space. When it's full, it's loud, and the tables are close enough that you'll hear the conversation next to you. It's not the place for a quiet catch-up.

Overall, The Little Fig is a great spot for a weekend breakfast if you don't mind a short wait. I'll definitely be going back for the hotcake.

Rating: 4.5 out of 5

How to save money as a teenager

Track where your money goes. For one month, write down everything you spend, even small things like a drink after school. Most people are surprised by how much disappears on snacks, delivery fees and in-app purchases.

Set a specific goal. Saving "some money" rarely works. Saving $400 for a new phone by the end of term gives you a reason to say no to impulse buys. Write the goal somewhere you'll see it.

Pay yourself first. When you get paid from a part-time job or receive birthday money, move a set amount into savings straight away, before you spend anything. Even 20 per cent adds up quickly.

Open a separate savings account. Many banks have accounts for under-18s with no fees and bonus interest if you don't withdraw. Keeping savings separate makes them harder to spend.

Wait before you buy. If you want something that isn't essential, wait 48 hours. If you still want it after two days, it's probably worth it. If you've forgotten about it, you've just saved money.

Cut the small leaks. Bring lunch from home a few days a week, unsubscribe from streaming services you don't use and turn off one-click payments in your games and apps.

Find ways to earn a bit more. Babysitting, mowing lawns, tutoring younger students and selling clothes you no longer wear can all bring in extra cash.

Saving isn't about never spending. It's about spending on the things that actually matter to you.

Should I learn coding?

Probably, yes, even if you don't plan to become a programmer.

Coding teaches you a way of thinking that's useful almost everywhere. You learn to break big problems into small steps, test your ideas and work out why something isn't working. These skills help in maths, science and even essay writing.

It's also increasingly useful in jobs that aren't technical. Scientists use code to analyse data, designers use it to build websites, and people in business use it to automate boring spreadsheet tasks. Knowing even a little can make you much more efficient and give you an edge when applying for jobs.

That said, coding isn't for everyone, and you don't need to force yourself if you really don't enjoy it. AI tools can now write simple code, so the job market for entry-level programmers is changing. The value is shifting from typing code to understanding problems and checking that solutions actually work.

If you want to try it, start with Python. It's easy to read, widely used and free. Websites like Codecademy, freeCodeCamp and CS50 from Harvard have good beginner courses. Pick a small project you care about, such as a quiz game or a program that tracks your study time, because building something real is far more motivating than doing exercises.

Give it a few weeks before deciding. Most people find the first few days frustrating, and then something clicks.

Reflection: Volunteering at the food bank

As part of our service learning program, I spent a Saturday volunteering at the Foodbank warehouse in Morningside. I expected it to be a bit boring, mostly packing boxes, but it changed the way I think about hunger in Australia.

The morning started with a short talk from the coordinator, Bev. She told us that more than three million Australians experienced food insecurity last year, and that many of them were working families, not just people who were homeless. That surprised me, because I'd always pictured food banks helping a very small group of people.

We spent most of the day sorting donated food. My job was checking use-by dates and separating tins, pasta and cereal into crates. It was repetitive, and after a few hours my back was sore. But Bev showed us how many families each pallet would feed, and seeing that number made the work feel more meaningful.

The part I remember most was talking to an older volunteer named Ray. He told me he'd used the food bank himself after losing his job, and that he'd volunteered every week since he got back on his feet.

Before this, I thought of helping as something you do for people who are very different from you. Ray made me realise that anyone can end up needing help.

I'd like to go back during the holidays, and I've already asked two friends if they want to come with me.
