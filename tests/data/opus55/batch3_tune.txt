Assistant Treasurer Stephen Jones said the change would make it easier for shoppers to compare products at a glance.

"At a time when families are watching every dollar, people deserve to be able to tell quickly which option is actually cheaper," he said.

Consumer group Choice, which has campaigned for stronger unit pricing rules for more than a decade, welcomed the decision. Its research found that many shoppers either couldn't find or couldn't read the unit price on existing labels, particularly on promotional tickets.

The Australian Food and Grocery Council said its members supported clearer labelling but warned that the changes would require significant work to update systems and signage across thousands of stores.

Smaller independent grocers with fewer than 10 stores will have an extra twelve months to comply. The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission will be responsible for enforcement and will be able to issue fines of up to $50,000 for repeated breaches.

To what extent did the Industrial Revolution improve living standards in Britain?

The Industrial Revolution eventually raised living standards in Britain to levels never seen before, but for many people living through it, especially in the first half of the nineteenth century, the benefits were slow to arrive and often outweighed by harsh conditions. Whether it "improved" life depends heavily on which group, and which period, is being considered.

In the long term, the gains are clear. Between 1750 and 1900, Britain's total output grew enormously, and by the late nineteenth century real wages for most workers were rising steadily. Mass production made goods such as cotton clothing, soap and household items far cheaper. Railways allowed fresh food to reach cities quickly, and by the 1870s education was becoming compulsory, opening opportunities that earlier generations never had.

However, for workers in the new industrial cities between about 1780 and 1850, life was often worse rather than better. Historians such as E. P. Thompson have argued that wages barely kept pace with rising food prices, while working hours were long and conditions dangerous. Children as young as six worked in mills and mines until the Factory Acts began to restrict this.

Living conditions in the cities were particularly grim. Rapid population growth led to overcrowded housing without proper sanitation, and diseases such as cholera and typhoid spread quickly. In Manchester in the 1840s, life expectancy for labourers was under twenty years.

Overall, the Industrial Revolution laid the foundations for higher living standards, but it took decades of reform and political pressure before most ordinary people shared in its benefits.

How does George Orwell use the character of Boxer in Animal Farm?

In Animal Farm, George Orwell uses Boxer the carthorse to represent the hard-working, loyal working class who believed in the promise of the revolution and were ultimately betrayed by it. Through Boxer's strength, devotion and tragic end, Orwell criticises both the ruling elite who exploited workers and the blind trust that allowed them to do so.

Boxer is the strongest animal on the farm and contributes more labour than anyone else. Orwell describes him as "as strong as any two ordinary horses put together", and he is admired by all the animals for his steady character. When problems arise, his response is always "I will work harder", which becomes his personal motto. This shows his commitment to the farm, but it also shows that he answers every difficulty by sacrificing more of himself rather than questioning why things are going wrong.

His second motto, "Napoleon is always right", reveals his greatest weakness. Boxer is not intelligent, and he relies on the pigs to think for him. Even when he feels uneasy about the executions or the changes to the commandments, he pushes his doubts aside. Orwell suggests that loyalty without critical thinking allows tyranny to grow.

Boxer's fate is the most shocking moment in the novel. After he collapses from overwork, the pigs claim to send him to a hospital, but the van that takes him belongs to a horse slaughterer. Squealer's lie about his death, and the pigs' purchase of whisky soon afterwards, make the betrayal complete.

Through Boxer, Orwell shows how those who give the most to a revolution can be the first to be sacrificed by it.

What is inflation and why does the Reserve Bank care about it?

Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices rises over time. If inflation is 3 per cent, something that cost $100 last year costs about $103 now. It doesn't mean every price goes up by the same amount; some things rise faster, and some even fall, but on average your money buys a bit less.

A small amount of inflation is considered healthy. The Reserve Bank of Australia aims to keep it between 2 and 3 per cent a year. When prices are rising gently, people and businesses are more willing to spend and invest, which keeps the economy growing.

Problems start when inflation gets too high or too low.

When it's too high, the cost of living rises faster than most people's wages, so households can afford less. Savings lose value, and businesses find it harder to plan because they don't know what their costs will be next year.

When it's too low, or prices actually fall, people may delay spending because they expect things to be cheaper later. That can slow the economy and lead to job losses.

The Reserve Bank's main tool is the cash rate, the interest rate it sets for overnight loans between banks. When inflation is too high, it raises the cash rate. Banks pass this on as higher interest rates on loans and mortgages, which encourages people to spend less and save more, slowing price rises. When inflation is too low, it does the opposite.

The trade-off is that higher rates can also slow the economy and push up unemployment, which is why the decisions are so closely watched.

How to write a good topic sentence

A topic sentence is the first sentence of a body paragraph. Its job is to tell the reader exactly what that paragraph will argue and how it connects to your overall thesis. If someone read only your topic sentences, they should be able to follow your whole argument.

A strong topic sentence usually has three parts:

1. The main idea of the paragraph, stated as a claim rather than a fact.
2. A link to the question or your thesis.
3. Often, a hint of the evidence or technique you'll discuss.

Compare these two:

Weak: "In To Kill a Mockingbird, Atticus defends Tom Robinson."
Strong: "Through Atticus's decision to defend Tom Robinson, Lee suggests that true courage means acting on your conscience even when you know you will lose."

The first just describes what happens. The second makes an argument that the paragraph can then prove.

Some common mistakes to avoid:
- Starting with a quote. Your own idea should come first, and the evidence after.
- Being too vague, such as "Lee uses many techniques to show her ideas."
- Repeating the essay question word for word without adding anything.

A useful test is to ask: could someone disagree with this sentence? If the answer is no, it's probably a fact rather than an argument, and you need to push it further.

Once your topic sentence is clear, the rest of the paragraph should explain and prove it, and the final sentence should link back to the question.

Excursion report: Year 10 Geography field trip to Moreton Bay

On 14 October, our class visited the Moreton Bay Marine Park to investigate the health of the mangrove ecosystem at Boondall Wetlands and the impact of human activity on the area.

We collected data at three sites along a transect running from the edge of the water to the boundary of the reserve. At each site we measured water salinity, soil pH, the number of crab burrows in a one-metre quadrat, and the density of mangrove seedlings. We also recorded any rubbish we found.

Our results showed that salinity decreased as we moved inland, from 32 parts per thousand at the water's edge to 18 at the third site. Crab burrow density was highest at the middle site, with an average of 41 burrows per square metre. Seedling density followed a similar pattern. The most common types of rubbish were plastic bottles and fishing line, mostly found at the first site.

These results suggest that the middle zone provides the best conditions for mangrove growth and crab populations, possibly because it is flooded regularly but not constantly. The rubbish near the water's edge is likely washed in by the tide, which shows that pollution from outside the wetlands still affects them.

One limitation of our investigation was that we only visited once, in spring, so our results may not reflect conditions at other times of the year. Visiting in different seasons would make the data more reliable.

Overall, the excursion showed that the wetlands are healthy but vulnerable, especially to plastic pollution.

Why do we have leap years?

We have leap years because it takes the Earth about 365.24 days to orbit the Sun, not exactly 365. That extra quarter of a day might not seem like much, but it adds up.

If every year had exactly 365 days, the calendar would drift about six hours out of line with the seasons each year. After 100 years, it would be out by almost a full month. Eventually, the middle of summer in Australia would fall in what the calendar calls June.

To fix this, we add an extra day, 29 February, roughly every four years. Four quarter-days make one whole day, so the calendar catches back up.

But the real number isn't exactly 365.25 either. It's slightly less, about 365.2422. Adding a leap day every four years overcorrects by about 11 minutes a year. Over centuries, that also adds up.

So there's an extra rule. Years divisible by 100 are not leap years, unless they are also divisible by 400. That's why 1900 wasn't a leap year, but 2000 was. The next year that breaks the normal pattern will be 2100.

This system is called the Gregorian calendar, introduced by Pope Gregory XIII in 1582. Before that, Europe used the Julian calendar, which had a leap year every four years without exception. By the 1500s it had drifted about ten days out of line, so when countries switched over, they skipped ten days entirely. In Britain, which switched in 1752, the date jumped straight from 2 September to 14 September.

The pros and cons of a four-day school week

Some schools in the United States and New Zealand have trialled a four-day week, and the idea comes up regularly in Australia. Here's a summary of the main arguments on each side.

Possible benefits:
- More time for rest. Students and teachers often report feeling less tired, which may help with mental health.
- Lower costs. Schools save on transport, cleaning and energy for the day they're closed.
- Teacher recruitment. In some areas, a four-day week has made it easier to attract and keep staff.
- Time for other activities, such as part-time work, sport, family time or catching up on study.

Possible drawbacks:
- Longer school days. Most trials extend the other four days to make up the hours, which can be exhausting, especially for younger students.
- Childcare problems. Working parents may need to find and pay for care on the extra day off.
- Effects on learning. Research from the US is mixed, but several studies have found small drops in achievement, especially in maths, when schools moved to four days.
- Unequal impact. Students from disadvantaged families may lose access to school meals, supervision and support on the day off.

Overall, the evidence suggests a four-day week can work in some situations, particularly in rural areas with long travel times or serious staffing shortages, but it isn't clearly better for learning. Any change would need careful planning and support for families who rely most on school.
