Corals on the southern Great Barrier Reef spawned up to two weeks earlier than usual last year, according to a new study that tracked spawning times over two decades.

Researchers from James Cook University and the Australian Institute of Marine Science analysed records from 1,200 coral colonies at Heron Island between 2004 and 2025. They found that the main spawning night, which normally falls a few days after the November full moon, has been shifting earlier as sea temperatures rise.

Lead author Dr Hannah Okafor said the change mattered because coral spawning is closely timed with other events on the reef.

"Coral larvae need the right conditions to settle and survive," she said. "If they're released when the water is too warm or the currents are different, fewer of them make it."

The study also found that spawning has become less synchronised. In some years colonies of the same species released eggs over several nights instead of one, which lowers the chance of fertilisation.

Not all the findings were negative. Some species showed little change, and the researchers said the reef's ability to adapt is still not fully understood.

The team plans to expand the monitoring to the northern reef next year. Okafor said volunteer divers and tourism operators had contributed many of the records and would play an important role in the next stage.

The research was published in the journal Coral Reefs on Monday.

Does technology make us lonelier?

It's easy to blame phones for loneliness. Walk into any café and you'll see people sitting together while staring at separate screens. But the link between technology and loneliness is more complicated than it looks, and the answer depends a lot on how technology is used.

There is real evidence that heavy social media use is linked with feeling lonely, particularly among teenagers. Scrolling through other people's highlights can make your own life seem dull by comparison. Time spent online can also replace time spent with people face to face, which tends to be more satisfying. Researchers have found that people who passively scroll report feeling worse than those who actively message or post.

On the other hand, technology connects people who would otherwise be isolated. A teenager who is the only person at their school interested in astronomy can find a community of thousands online. Elderly people living alone can video-call their grandchildren. During the COVID-19 lockdowns, technology was the main way many people stayed in touch at all.

The difference seems to lie in whether technology adds to our relationships or replaces them. Using a group chat to organise a weekend catch-up builds connection. Spending three hours watching strangers' videos alone in your room probably doesn't.

So technology itself doesn't make us lonely. The way we use it can. Like most tools, it can bring people closer or push them apart, and the choice is mostly ours.

What caused the First World War?

The First World War began in 1914, but its causes had been building for decades. Historians often summarise them with the acronym MAIN: militarism, alliances, imperialism and nationalism. Each played a part, and together they turned a regional crisis into a world war.

Militarism meant that the major powers had been building up their armies and navies for years. Germany and Britain were locked in a naval arms race, and most countries had detailed war plans ready to go. This made leaders more willing to use force and less patient with diplomacy.

The alliance system divided Europe into two armed camps. The Triple Alliance linked Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy, while the Triple Entente linked Britain, France and Russia. These agreements were meant to keep the peace by making war too risky, but they also meant that a conflict between two countries could quickly drag in the rest.

Imperialism created rivalry as European powers competed for colonies in Africa and Asia. Disputes over territory, such as the Moroccan crises, increased tension between Germany and France.

Nationalism was perhaps the most powerful force. Pride in one's nation made people eager for war, and in the Balkans it fuelled demands for independence from Austria-Hungary.

The spark came on 28 June 1914, when Archduke Franz Ferdinand was assassinated in Sarajevo by a Bosnian Serb nationalist. Within six weeks, the alliance system had pulled most of Europe into war.

Loneliness in Of Mice and Men

John Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men shows how loneliness affects almost everyone on the ranch. Through characters such as Candy, Crooks and Curley's wife, Steinbeck suggests that isolation was a common experience for people living on the margins during the Great Depression.

George and Lennie are unusual because they travel together. As George tells Lennie, "Guys like us, that work on ranches, are the loneliest guys in the world." Their friendship and their dream of owning a farm give them something the other workers lack. This makes the loneliness of the other characters stand out even more.

Crooks is isolated because of his race. He lives alone in the harness room and is not allowed in the bunkhouse. When Lennie visits him, Crooks admits that "a guy goes nuts if he ain't got nobody." His bitterness shows how prejudice has cut him off from the companionship he desperately wants.

Curley's wife is lonely in a different way. She is the only woman on the ranch and is never given a name, which shows how little the men think of her. She tries to talk to the workers, but they avoid her because they fear Curley. Her conversation with Lennie in the barn shows her longing to be heard.

Steinbeck uses these characters to show that loneliness is not just a personal problem. It is created by the harsh social and economic conditions of the time, which kept people moving, divided and afraid.

How do vaccines work?

Vaccines work by training your immune system to recognise a germ before you actually get sick from it.

Your immune system protects you from viruses and bacteria. When a new germ enters your body, the immune system has to figure out what it is and how to fight it. This takes time, often a week or more, and during that time the germ can make you very ill.

A vaccine gives your immune system a preview. It contains something that looks like the germ but can't cause the disease. Depending on the vaccine, this might be a weakened or killed version of the germ, a small piece of it such as a protein from its surface, or instructions (as in mRNA vaccines) that tell your cells to make that protein for a short time.

Your immune system responds to this preview by making antibodies, which are proteins that stick to the germ and help destroy it. It also creates memory cells. These cells stay in your body for months or years and remember what the germ looks like.

If the real germ turns up later, the memory cells recognise it straight away. Your body can produce antibodies quickly, often stopping the infection before you feel sick, or making the illness much milder.

Some vaccines need booster doses because the memory fades over time, or because the germ changes, as the flu virus does each year. When enough people in a community are vaccinated, it also becomes harder for diseases to spread, which protects people who can't be vaccinated.

Why is the sky blue?

The sky looks blue because of the way sunlight interacts with the air.

Sunlight looks white, but it's actually a mix of all the colours of the rainbow. Each colour has a different wavelength. Red light has the longest wavelength, and blue and violet light have the shortest.

When sunlight enters the atmosphere, it hits tiny gas molecules, mostly nitrogen and oxygen. These molecules scatter the light in all directions. Shorter wavelengths are scattered much more strongly than longer ones. This effect is called Rayleigh scattering, after the British scientist who explained it.

Because blue light is scattered so much, it reaches your eyes from every part of the sky, not just from the direction of the sun. So wherever you look, you see blue.

You might wonder why the sky isn't violet, since violet has an even shorter wavelength. There are a few reasons. The sun gives off less violet light than blue, some violet light is absorbed high in the atmosphere, and our eyes are more sensitive to blue.

The same idea explains red sunsets. When the sun is low on the horizon, its light has to travel through much more air to reach you. Most of the blue light is scattered away before it arrives, leaving the reds and oranges.

On the Moon, which has no atmosphere, there's nothing to scatter the light, so the sky is black even during the day.

Compound interest is interest that you earn on your interest, not just on the money you originally put in.

Say you put $1,000 into a savings account that pays 5% interest a year. After the first year, you earn $50, so you have $1,050. With simple interest, you'd earn another $50 in the second year. With compound interest, you earn 5% of $1,050, which is $52.50. The extra $2.50 is interest on last year's interest.

That doesn't sound like much, but it adds up over time. After 10 years, your $1,000 would grow to about $1,629. After 30 years, it would be about $4,322, even though you never added another cent.

The two things that matter most are the interest rate and time. A higher rate makes your money grow faster, but time is usually the bigger factor. Someone who starts saving $50 a week at 20 will end up with far more at 60 than someone who starts saving $100 a week at 40, because their money has twice as long to compound.

How often interest is added also makes a small difference. Interest that compounds monthly grows slightly faster than interest that compounds once a year.

The same principle works against you with debt. Credit card interest compounds too, which is why an unpaid balance can grow quickly. If you only make the minimum repayment, you can end up paying back far more than you borrowed.

The simple lesson is to start saving early and pay off high-interest debt as fast as you can.

How to study for exams without burning out

Start early. Cramming the night before feels productive, but you forget most of it within days. If you start reviewing two or three weeks out, you can spread the work into shorter sessions and give your brain time to store the information properly.

Test yourself instead of rereading. Rereading your notes feels easy, which is exactly the problem. You recognise the material, so you think you know it. Practice questions, flashcards and past papers force you to actually recall information, and that's what builds memory.

Mix up your subjects. Spending a whole day on chemistry is less effective than doing an hour of chemistry, an hour of history and an hour of maths. Switching topics is harder, but it helps you remember more.

Take proper breaks. Try working for 25 to 45 minutes, then stopping for 5 to 10. Get up, walk around and don't check your phone if you can help it. Your concentration will last much longer.

Sleep. This is the one most students skip, and it matters more than almost anything else. Your brain consolidates what you've learned while you sleep. Pulling an all-nighter before an exam usually does more harm than good.

Ask for help early. If there's a topic you don't understand, ask your teacher a week before the exam, not the morning of.

Finally, be realistic. You don't need to study ten hours a day. Consistent, focused work over a few weeks will get you much further.

Tips for your first job interview

Research the business. Spend twenty minutes on their website and social media before you go. Know what they sell or do, and think of one reason you'd like to work there. Interviewers notice when someone has made the effort.

Practise common questions out loud. You'll almost certainly be asked to tell them about yourself, why you want the job, and how you'd handle a difficult customer. Practise your answers with a family member or in front of a mirror. You don't need to memorise them, just get comfortable talking.

Use examples, even if you haven't had a job before. Schoolwork, sport, volunteering and helping at home all count. If they ask about teamwork, talk about a group project or your netball team. If they ask about reliability, mention that you've never missed a training session.

Dress one step above what staff normally wear. For a café or supermarket, neat pants and a clean, plain top are usually fine.

Arrive about ten minutes early. Not thirty, and definitely not late. If something goes wrong, call ahead.

Have a question ready for the end. Something like "What does a normal shift look like?" or "What do you like about working here?" shows you're genuinely interested.

Follow up. A short thank-you email or message the same day can set you apart.

Don't worry if you're nervous. Interviewers expect it, especially for a first job. Smile, be honest and be yourself.

These are the pancakes I make every Sunday, and they've never let me down.

They're fluffy, slightly golden on the outside and soft in the middle, and you probably already have everything you need. The trick is not to overmix the batter. A few lumps are fine. They'll disappear as the pancakes cook.

Ingredients (makes about 10)
1 1/2 cups self-raising flour
2 tablespoons sugar
1 1/4 cups milk
1 egg
2 tablespoons melted butter, plus extra for the pan
1 teaspoon vanilla extract

Method
Whisk the flour and sugar together in a large bowl and make a well in the centre.

In a jug, whisk the milk, egg, melted butter and vanilla.

Pour the wet ingredients into the well and stir gently with a fork until just combined. Let the batter rest for five minutes while the pan heats up.

Heat a non-stick frying pan over medium heat and add a little butter. Pour in about a quarter cup of batter for each pancake.

Cook until bubbles appear on the surface and the edges look set, about two minutes. Flip and cook for another minute until golden.

Keep cooked pancakes warm in a low oven while you make the rest.

Serve with maple syrup, berries and a dollop of yoghurt, or go classic with lemon and sugar. Leftover batter keeps in the fridge for a day, but it won't rise quite as much.

Is it worth learning a second language?

Yes, for most people it is, though maybe not for the reasons you'd expect.

The practical benefits are real but sometimes overstated. Speaking another language can help with some jobs, especially in tourism, international business and government. It makes travel easier and more rewarding. But with translation apps getting better every year, being able to order food or ask for directions isn't as strong an argument as it used to be.

The bigger benefits are harder to measure. Learning a language changes how you understand your own. Many people only really grasp English grammar once they've had to learn the grammar of another language. You also start to notice that some ideas don't translate neatly, which shows you how much language shapes the way people think.

There's also the connection with people. Talking to someone in their first language, even badly, is very different from talking through an app. If you have family who speak another language, learning it can bring you closer to grandparents and relatives in a way nothing else can.

Research also suggests that learning a language is good exercise for the brain, improving memory and attention, although the size of these effects is still debated.

The honest catch is that it takes a long time. You need years of regular practice to become fluent. If you're going to start, pick a language you have a real reason to use, whether that's family, travel or a culture you love.
