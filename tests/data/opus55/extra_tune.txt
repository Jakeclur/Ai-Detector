"You've got her chin." He nodded at the suitcase. "She talked about you. Said you'd come back eventually."

"The cat's still there," he said. "Mrs Hale's been feeding him. He's enormous now. Answers to Boris, if he feels like it."

Nadia laughed, and it came out wobblier than she meant it to.

By the time the ferry finally appeared, a white shape growing against the grey, she had learned that the roof leaked in the north-east corner, that the lemon tree still fruited every winter, and that her grandmother had kept every one of her school drawings in a biscuit tin under the bed.

Hobart City Council has approved a plan to turn a disused rail corridor in the city's north into a 4.2-kilometre walking and cycling path, ending more than a decade of debate over the land's future.

The council voted 9 to 3 on Monday night to fund the first stage of the project, which will connect the suburbs of Moonah and Glenorchy with a sealed shared path, lighting and new crossings at four major roads. Construction is expected to begin in early 2027 and take about 18 months.

The corridor has been unused since freight services stopped in 2014. Since then, proposals have ranged from light rail to housing, and a community group has campaigned for a permanent green space along the route.

Lord Mayor Anna Reynolds said the decision was "a practical step that doesn't rule anything out". The design will leave enough room for a future light rail line if state funding becomes available.

"People have been walking along this corridor informally for years," she said. "This makes it safe, accessible and something the whole community can use."

The first stage will cost an estimated $11.6 million, with $7 million coming from a federal active transport grant and the rest from council funds.

Not everyone supports the plan. Councillor Mark Dawson, who voted against it, said the money would be better spent on road maintenance and that the council had underestimated ongoing costs. Some residents whose properties back onto the corridor have also raised concerns about privacy and lighting at night.

The council says it will hold public consultation on the final design later this year.

Australian researchers have found that a common garden weed may help clean up soil contaminated with heavy metals, offering a cheaper alternative to removing and replacing polluted earth.

The study, published this week by a team from the University of Newcastle, tested several fast-growing plant species on soil taken from former industrial sites in the Hunter region. One species, a type of saltbush usually treated as a weed, absorbed significantly more lead and cadmium through its roots than the others and stored the metals in its leaves.

Over two growing seasons, plots planted with the saltbush showed a 30 to 40 per cent drop in available lead in the top layer of soil, compared with almost no change in unplanted plots.

Lead author Dr Sarah Lim said the approach, known as phytoremediation, was not new, but finding a hardy local plant that worked in Australian conditions was promising.

"Digging up contaminated soil and trucking it to landfill is incredibly expensive and just moves the problem somewhere else," she said. "If plants can do some of that work for us, it could make clean-up possible on sites that would otherwise be left alone."

The plants themselves must be harvested and disposed of safely, because the metals stay in the leaves. The team is now looking at whether the harvested material could be processed to recover the metals.

Other experts cautioned that the results came from small test plots and that larger field trials were needed. Professor David Grant, a soil scientist not involved in the study, said phytoremediation tends to work slowly and is best suited to mildly contaminated land rather than heavily polluted sites.

How does Shakespeare present the theme of ambition in Macbeth?

In Macbeth, Shakespeare presents ambition as a force that is natural in itself but becomes destructive when it is separated from conscience. Through Macbeth's rise and fall, and through the contrast with Lady Macbeth and Banquo, the play suggests that unchecked ambition corrupts both the individual and the world around them.

At the start of the play, Macbeth's ambition is presented as something he is aware of and uneasy about. When the witches predict he will be king, he immediately imagines murder, but the thought horrifies him: his "seated heart knock[s] at [his] ribs". In his soliloquy in Act 1 Scene 7, he admits that he has "no spur" to prick the sides of his intent, "but only vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself". The image of a rider who jumps too hard and falls on the other side foreshadows his downfall and shows that, at this point, he understands the danger.

Lady Macbeth plays a key role in pushing his ambition past his conscience. She calls on spirits to "unsex me here" and fill her "from the crown to the toe top-full of direst cruelty", rejecting the qualities she associates with kindness. She attacks Macbeth's masculinity until he agrees to the murder. Shakespeare suggests that ambition becomes more dangerous when it is encouraged by others.

Banquo provides a contrast. He hears a prophecy that his descendants will be kings, but he does not act on it, and he asks for help to resist "cursed thoughts". His restraint shows that ambition is not automatically evil; what matters is whether a person chooses to act on it.

By the end of the play, Macbeth's ambition has left him isolated and numb. His famous "tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow" speech presents life as meaningless, "a tale told by an idiot". Shakespeare uses this collapse to show the cost of ambition without moral limits.

To what extent was the Great Depression responsible for the rise of the Nazi Party in Germany?

The Great Depression was the most important single factor in the Nazi Party's rise to power, but it was not the only one. The economic crisis turned a small extremist party into a mass movement, yet the Nazis also benefited from long-term weaknesses in the Weimar Republic, widespread resentment of the Treaty of Versailles, and the decisions of conservative elites who believed they could control Hitler.

The impact of the Depression on Nazi support is clear from the election results. In 1928, before the crash, the Nazis won just 2.6 per cent of the vote. By July 1932, with unemployment above six million, they won 37.3 per cent and became the largest party in the Reichstag. Mass unemployment, business failures and falling farm prices left many Germans desperate, and the Nazis offered simple explanations and promises of work and national renewal.

However, the Depression alone does not explain why Germans turned to the Nazis rather than to other parties. The Weimar Republic had never fully gained legitimacy. Many Germans associated it with the humiliation of Versailles, the hyperinflation of 1923 and political instability. Proportional representation produced weak coalition governments, and by 1930 Chancellor Brüning was governing by presidential decree, which weakened democracy before Hitler came to power.

The role of political elites was also crucial. Hitler did not seize power through an election victory; in November 1932 the Nazi vote actually fell. He was appointed Chancellor in January 1933 after conservative politicians, including Franz von Papen, persuaded President Hindenburg that Hitler could be used and contained.

In conclusion, the Depression created the conditions for the Nazis' rise by making their message appealing to millions of Germans. But without the existing weaknesses of the Weimar system and the miscalculations of conservative leaders, economic crisis alone would not have brought Hitler to power.

Should voting be compulsory? Write a discussion essay.

Australia is one of a small number of countries where voting is compulsory, and the system is often held up as a model of democratic participation. Supporters argue that it produces governments that reflect the whole population, while critics say that forcing people to vote undermines individual freedom. Both sides raise valid points, but on balance compulsory voting does more to strengthen democracy than to weaken it.

The main argument for compulsory voting is that it leads to very high turnout. Around 90 per cent of enrolled Australians vote in federal elections, compared with roughly 60 per cent in countries such as the United States and the United Kingdom. When almost everyone votes, election results are less likely to be decided by small, highly motivated groups, and politicians must appeal to a broad range of people rather than just their most loyal supporters.

Compulsory voting may also reduce some of the barriers that stop disadvantaged groups from voting. Because elections are held on Saturdays and polling places are everywhere, voting becomes a normal civic routine rather than a special effort.

On the other hand, critics argue that a democracy should include the freedom not to participate. Some people object to being fined for staying home, and others worry that people who are uninterested or uninformed will vote randomly, adding noise to the result. The rate of informal votes, ballots that are left blank or filled in incorrectly, suggests that some people do turn up only to avoid a fine.

However, voters are only required to attend and have their name marked off. They can still choose not to fill in the ballot, so the system protects freedom more than its critics suggest.

Overall, while compulsory voting is not perfect, its benefits for representation and participation outweigh its costs.

How to set up two-factor authentication on most accounts

Two-factor authentication (2FA) adds a second step when you log in, so someone who steals your password still can't get into your account. It takes about five minutes per account and is one of the most effective things you can do for your online security.

1. Choose an authenticator app. Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator and Authy are all free. They're safer than text-message codes, which can be intercepted if someone takes over your phone number.

2. Find the security settings. In most accounts this is under Settings, then Security or Privacy. Look for "Two-factor authentication", "2-step verification" or "Login approvals".

3. Scan the QR code. The site will show a QR code. Open your authenticator app, tap the plus button and scan it. The app will start showing a six-digit code that changes every 30 seconds.

4. Enter the code. Type the current code into the website to confirm it's working.

5. Save your backup codes. Most sites give you a list of one-time backup codes. Print them or store them in a password manager. If you lose your phone, these are how you get back in.

Start with your most important accounts: email first, because it can be used to reset everything else, then banking, then social media.

One tip: if you get a 2FA prompt you didn't request, don't approve it. It usually means someone has your password, so change it straight away.

Lab report: The effect of temperature on the rate of enzyme activity

Aim
To investigate how temperature affects the rate at which the enzyme catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide.

Hypothesis
The rate of reaction will increase as temperature rises up to about 37 °C, then decrease at higher temperatures as the enzyme denatures.

Method
Potato discs of equal size were used as a source of catalase. For each temperature (10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 °C), five discs were placed in a test tube of 3% hydrogen peroxide in a water bath. The oxygen produced was collected in an inverted measuring cylinder, and the volume was recorded after two minutes. Each temperature was tested three times and the results averaged.

Results
The average volume of oxygen rose from 4.2 mL at 10 °C to a peak of 17.8 mL at 40 °C. It then fell sharply to 9.1 mL at 50 °C and 2.3 mL at 60 °C.

Discussion
The results mostly support the hypothesis. As temperature increased, particles moved faster and collided more often, so more enzyme-substrate complexes formed. The peak occurred at 40 °C rather than 37 °C, which may be because potato catalase has a slightly different optimum from human enzymes, or because the water bath temperature varied by about 2 °C. Above 40 °C, the enzyme's active site changed shape and could no longer bind to hydrogen peroxide, so the rate dropped.

One limitation was that the discs were cut by hand and may not have had exactly the same surface area. Using a cork borer and a scalpel guide would improve accuracy.

Conclusion
Temperature affects enzyme activity, with an optimum around 40 °C for potato catalase.

Why your code works on your computer but not on the server

This is one of the most common and frustrating problems in software, and it almost always comes down to differences between the two environments rather than a bug in your logic.

The usual suspects:

Different versions. Your laptop might be running Python 3.12 while the server has 3.9, or a library might have updated between when you installed it and when the server did. A function that exists in one version may be missing or behave differently in another. Pinning exact versions in a requirements or lock file fixes most of these.

Missing environment variables. API keys, database URLs and feature flags often live in a .env file on your machine that never gets copied to the server. The code then fails when it tries to read a value that isn't there.

File paths. Paths like C:\Users\me\data.csv or ~/projects/app/data won't exist on the server. Use paths relative to the project, and remember that Linux servers treat File.txt and file.txt as different files.

Permissions. The server process might not be allowed to write to the folder where your code saves files or logs.

Network access. Your laptop can reach your local database, but the server might be blocked by a firewall.

The fastest way to narrow it down is to read the actual error message on the server, not just the fact that it failed. If there's no error, add logging around the part that breaks. Containers like Docker exist largely to solve this problem by making the two environments identical.

Book review: The Midnight Library by Matt Haig

The premise of The Midnight Library is irresistible. Nora Seed, at the lowest point of her life, finds herself in a library between life and death, where every book is a different version of her life, one where she made a different choice. She can try any of them.

What follows is part fantasy, part self-help novel. Nora becomes a glaciologist in the Arctic, a rock star, an Olympic swimmer, a wife and mother. Each life offers something her real one lacks, and each one disappoints her in a new way. The structure is clever, and the pace is quick. Haig keeps the chapters short, and it's hard not to read "just one more".

The book's strength is its warmth. Haig writes about depression with honesty and without melodrama, and Nora is easy to care about. The central idea, that regret is built on an imaginary perfect alternative, is simple but genuinely comforting.

Its weakness is that it sometimes feels like it's explaining its lesson rather than letting the story carry it. Characters, especially the librarian Mrs Elm, occasionally deliver lines that sound more like inspirational quotes than conversation. Some of Nora's alternate lives are sketched so quickly that they feel like ideas rather than places.

Still, it's a hopeful, easy-to-read book that a lot of people will find meaningful, particularly anyone going through a hard time. It's not subtle, but it isn't trying to be.

Rating: 3.5 out of 5.
