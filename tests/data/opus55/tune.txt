Sunlight looks white, but it's actually a mix of every colour. When it enters the atmosphere, it runs into gas molecules, mostly nitrogen and oxygen, that are far smaller than the wavelength of visible light. Molecules that small scatter short wavelengths much more strongly than long ones. Blue light has a short wavelength, so it gets bounced around the sky in every direction, while red and orange light mostly pass straight through.

So when you look at any part of the sky away from the sun, you're seeing blue light that has been scattered towards your eyes. This effect is called Rayleigh scattering.

You might wonder why the sky isn't violet, since violet has an even shorter wavelength. There are two reasons. The sun gives off less violet light than blue, and our eyes are much more sensitive to blue. The mix that reaches us reads as sky blue.

The same idea explains sunsets. When the sun is low, its light travels through far more air to reach you. Most of the blue gets scattered away along the path, leaving the reds and oranges.

A vaccine trains your immune system to recognise a germ before you ever meet the real thing.

Your immune system learns by example. When a virus or bacterium gets into your body, it carries molecules on its surface called antigens. Immune cells spot these, build antibodies that stick to them, and keep a set of memory cells on file. The first time this happens it's slow, which is why you get sick. The second time, the memory cells react within days or even hours, often before you notice any symptoms.

A vaccine gives your body that first lesson safely. Depending on the type, it contains:

- a weakened or inactivated version of the germ
- just one piece of it, such as a surface protein
- instructions (mRNA) that get your own cells to make that protein for a short time

None of these can cause the full disease, but they're enough for your immune system to build antibodies and memory cells.

That's also why some vaccines need boosters. Memory can fade over time, and a booster reminds the immune system what to look for. When enough people in a community are immune, the germ struggles to spread, which protects people who can't be vaccinated.

Compound interest means you earn interest on your interest, not just on the money you started with.

Say you put $1,000 in a savings account that pays 5% a year. After one year you have $1,050. In the second year, the 5% is worked out on $1,050, not $1,000, so you earn $52.50 instead of $50. The gap looks tiny at first, but it keeps growing because every year's interest becomes part of the base for the next year.

After 10 years, simple interest would give you $1,500. Compound interest gives you about $1,629. After 30 years it's $2,500 versus roughly $4,322.

Three things control how fast it grows:

1. The interest rate. A higher rate compounds much faster.
2. Time. This is the big one. Most of the growth happens in the later years.
3. How often interest is added. Monthly compounding beats yearly compounding slightly.

The same maths works against you with debt. Credit card balances compound too, which is why a small unpaid balance can turn into a big one.

A handy shortcut is the rule of 72: divide 72 by the interest rate to estimate how many years it takes to double your money. At 6%, that's about 12 years.

Photosynthesis is how plants make their own food using light.

The overall equation is:

carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (using light energy)

6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

It happens inside chloroplasts, mostly in the leaf cells. Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, a green pigment that absorbs red and blue light and reflects green, which is why leaves look green.

There are two main stages.

Light-dependent reactions. These happen in the thylakoid membranes. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy, which is used to split water molecules. This releases oxygen as a by-product and produces two energy carriers, ATP and NADPH.

Light-independent reactions (the Calvin cycle). These happen in the stroma, the fluid around the thylakoids. The plant uses the ATP and NADPH to turn carbon dioxide into glucose.

Plants use the glucose for energy in respiration, store it as starch, or turn it into cellulose to build cell walls.

The rate of photosynthesis depends on light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature. If any one of these is in short supply, it limits the rate, even if the other two are plentiful. These are called limiting factors, and they come up a lot in exam questions.

The methods that work best are the ones that make your brain do some work, not the ones that feel productive.

Test yourself instead of rereading. Rereading notes feels comfortable, but it mostly builds familiarity, not memory. Close the book and try to write down or say out loud what you know, then check. Past papers and flashcards are great for this.

Space it out. Studying a topic for 30 minutes on four different days beats two hours the night before. Each time you come back after a gap, you have to work a bit to remember, and that effort is what makes it stick.

Mix topics up. If you're doing maths, don't do twenty of the same question in a row. Mixing question types forces you to work out which method to use, which is exactly what the exam asks you to do.

Explain it to someone. If you can teach a concept to a friend or a younger sibling, you understand it. If you get stuck halfway, you've found a gap.

Sleep. Memory gets consolidated while you sleep, so an all-nighter usually costs more than it gains.

A simple plan: list your topics, rate how confident you feel about each, and spend most of your time on the weak ones, testing yourself as you go.

- Want to make websites or web apps? JavaScript.
- Interested in data, AI, games with Pygame, or general problem-solving? Python.
- Not sure yet? Python.

The causes of World War I are usually grouped into four long-term factors: militarism, alliances, imperialism and nationalism. In the decades before 1914, the major European powers built up huge armies and navies, and the naval race between Britain and Germany in particular created deep suspicion. At the same time, Europe split into two alliance blocs, the Triple Entente of Britain, France and Russia, and the Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy, which meant a local conflict could quickly drag in every major power. Competition for colonies in Africa and Asia added to the rivalry, while nationalism fuelled tension in the Balkans, where Slavic groups wanted independence from Austria-Hungary. The immediate trigger came on 28 June 1914, when Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb nationalist, assassinated Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo. Austria-Hungary, backed by Germany, issued an ultimatum to Serbia, and within weeks the alliance system had turned a regional crisis into a continental war. Historians still debate how much blame each country deserves, but most agree that the assassination only lit a fuse that had been laid over many years.

In To Kill a Mockingbird, Harper Lee shows that real courage is not about physical strength or winning, but about doing what is right when you know you will probably lose.

Lee makes this idea explicit through Mrs Dubose. Jem thinks she is just a cruel old woman, but after her death Atticus reveals she was fighting a morphine addiction so she could die "beholden to nothing and nobody." He tells Jem that real courage is "when you know you're licked before you begin but you begin anyway and you see it through no matter what." This shifts Jem's view of bravery away from the image of a man with a gun.

That contrast matters because Atticus earlier shoots a rabid dog with a single shot, and the children are thrilled. Yet Atticus himself does not treat this as courage. By placing these moments side by side, Lee suggests that the quiet, moral kind of bravery is harder and more important than the dramatic kind.

The gold rushes of the 1850s transformed the Australian colonies in almost every way.

The most obvious change was population. When gold was found near Bathurst in 1851 and then at Ballarat and Bendigo in Victoria, people poured in from Britain, Ireland, Europe, America and China. Australia's non-Indigenous population roughly tripled in a decade, from about 430,000 in 1851 to over 1.1 million by 1861. Melbourne grew from a small town into one of the richest cities in the British Empire.

The economy changed too. Gold made the colonies wealthy and funded railways, telegraph lines, public buildings and universities. It also pushed up wages and drew workers away from farms and towns, at least for a while.

The rushes also shaped politics. Miners resented the expensive licence fee and the harsh way it was enforced, and in 1854 tensions boiled over at the Eureka Stockade near Ballarat. The rebellion was crushed, but the reforms that followed gave miners the vote and helped push Victoria towards democratic rights such as the secret ballot.

There was a darker side. Chinese miners faced violent attacks, such as the Lambing Flat riots, and restrictive laws that later fed into the White Australia policy. For Aboriginal people, the rush brought another wave of dispossession, as miners and settlers moved onto their land and destroyed water sources and food supplies.

Australia has moved quickly on renewable energy over the past decade, but it still has a long way to go.

Renewables now supply around 40% of the electricity on the main national grid, up from about 15% in 2015. Rooftop solar is a big part of that story. Roughly one in three Australian homes has panels, one of the highest rates in the world. Large wind and solar farms have also expanded, especially in South Australia, which regularly runs on close to 100% renewable power for hours at a time.

The federal target is 82% renewable electricity by 2030. Getting there depends on three things:

Transmission. New wind and solar farms are often far from cities, and the poles and wires to connect them are expensive and slow to build. Several major projects are running years behind schedule and over budget.

Storage. Solar produces nothing at night, so the grid needs big batteries and pumped hydro, such as the delayed Snowy 2.0 project, to fill the gaps.

Replacing coal. Several coal power stations are scheduled to close this decade. If replacements aren't ready in time, there's a risk of supply shortfalls and price spikes.

Outside electricity, progress is slower. Transport, heavy industry and agriculture still rely heavily on fossil fuels, and these sectors will be harder to change than the power grid.

That error means you're trying to get an item at a position that doesn't exist in the list.

Python lists start counting from 0, so a list with 3 items has positions 0, 1 and 2:

fruits = ["apple", "banana", "cherry"]
print(fruits[3])   # IndexError: list index out of range

The most common causes are:

1. Off-by-one loops. Using range(len(fruits) + 1) or range(1, len(fruits) + 1) goes one step too far. Use range(len(fruits)), or better, loop over the items directly:

for fruit in fruits:
    print(fruit)

2. An empty list. If the list is empty, even fruits[0] fails. Check first with if fruits: before accessing items.

3. Changing a list while looping over it. Removing items inside a loop shifts the positions, so later indexes no longer exist.

4. Assuming input has a certain length. For example, splitting a line of text and expecting three parts when there are only two.

To find the problem, print the length of the list and the index just before the line that crashes:

print(len(fruits), i)

That usually shows exactly where the numbers stop lining up. If you paste the part of your code with the loop, I can point to the exact line.

A few changes make a much bigger difference than willpower alone.

Check your numbers first. Open the screen time settings on your phone and look at which apps take up the most time. Most people are surprised by one or two of them. That tells you where to focus.

Make the habit harder to start:
- Move the worst apps off your home screen, or into a folder on the last page.
- Log out of them, so you have to type your password each time.
- Turn off all notifications except messages from actual people.

Set limits that do the deciding for you. Use the app timer feature to cap your top apps at, say, 30 minutes a day. When the timer hits, you have to make a conscious choice to keep going.

Change your environment. Charge your phone outside your bedroom overnight and use a cheap alarm clock instead. Keep it in your bag, not your pocket, while you're doing homework.

Replace, don't just remove. If you scroll when you're bored, have something else ready: a book on your bedside table, a guitar in the corner, a walk with a friend. It's much easier to swap a habit than to stop one.

Start small. Cutting an hour a day straight away usually doesn't last. Try 20 minutes less this week, then a bit more next week.
