The bakery on Fitzroy Street had been closed for three years when the lights came on again.

Lena noticed it on her way home from the train, a warm yellow square in a row of dark shopfronts. Through the fogged-up window she could see someone moving behind the counter, an old woman in a blue apron, sliding trays into the oven as if she'd never stopped.

Lena's feet slowed without her deciding. The smell reached her before she got to the door: butter and cinnamon and something burnt at the edges, exactly the way it used to smell when she was little and her dad bought her a scroll every Saturday.

The bell above the door still rang. The woman looked up. "We're not open yet, love. Tomorrow."

"Sorry. I just — I used to come here. With my dad."

The woman studied her for a moment, flour on her hands. "Saturday mornings," she said slowly. "Cinnamon scroll. You always picked the one with the most icing."

Lena stared at her. "How do you remember that?"

"I remember everyone's order. It's the only thing my memory's still good for." The woman wiped her hands on her apron. "Mr Hartley bought it, after I sold up. Lasted six months. Place has been empty since. I got sick of walking past it."

"So you just... came back?"

"Rented it again last month. Don't tell my kids. They think I'm at bowls."

She reached under the counter, wrapped something in a paper bag and held it out.

It was still warm. It had the most icing.

Mira had been a lighthouse keeper for nine years when the ship that didn't exist ran aground on the rocks below.

She saw it at dawn, a tall, three-masted thing with grey sails, tilted on the reef where the tide had left it. There were no ships like that anymore. There hadn't been for a hundred years.

She radioed the coast guard. The operator, a young man who sounded half-asleep, said there was nothing on their system. "Are you sure it's not a cloud bank?"

Mira looked at the ship. A rope swung loose from its side, knocking against the hull with a hollow sound she could hear from the cliff. "I'm sure."

By the time she climbed down the path to the beach, the tide had turned. Up close, the ship was worse: its wood was silvered and soft with age, barnacles crusted its keel, and there was no one on the deck. On the bow, half worn away, she could make out a name. Endurance.

That night she looked it up on the lighthouse's old computer. An Endurance had sailed from Hobart in 1898 and was never seen again. Twenty-two crew. A storm, the records said.

She didn't sleep. She sat in the lamp room, watching the dark shape on the rocks while the beam swept over it every eight seconds.

At three in the morning, the beam passed across the reef and found nothing but water.

In the morning there was only one thing on the sand: a brass compass, green with age, its needle pointing steadily back towards the lighthouse.

Tim's grandmother didn't believe in phones, which was why she was the only person in the family who knew what was really going on.

"You all stare at those screens at dinner," she said, "and you miss everything."

She was right, as it turned out. While everyone else scrolled, she noticed that Tim's sister had stopped eating breakfast and that his dad came home later every week. She noticed that Tim's mum laughed less. And she noticed that Tim, who was fourteen, had started wearing long sleeves in summer.

She didn't say anything at first. She just started asking him to help her in the garden on Sunday afternoons.

It was boring work, mostly. Pulling weeds, staking tomatoes, moving the hose from one bed to another. She didn't ask questions. She talked about her own childhood, about a dog she'd had called Biscuit, about the year the creek flooded and they lost all the chickens.

On the fourth Sunday, while they were kneeling in the dirt, Tim said, "Things aren't great at school."

His grandmother kept pulling weeds. "No?"

"There are these guys. It's been going on for a while."

"Mm." She sat back on her heels and looked at him properly for the first time that afternoon. "Thank you for telling me."

That was all she said, at first. But that night she rang his mum, and the next week there were meetings, and teachers, and a school counsellor with very kind eyes.

On Sunday, Tim went out to the garden anyway. His grandmother was already there, holding out a second pair of gloves.

Melbourne's public transport network will move to a contactless payment system next year, allowing passengers to tap on with bank cards and phones instead of a myki card, the Victorian government has confirmed.

The change will begin on trams and trains in the inner city from March and expand to the entire network, including regional buses, by the end of 2027. Existing myki cards will continue to work during the transition.

Public Transport Minister Gabrielle Williams said the move would make travel simpler for visitors and occasional users, who often had to buy a card before they could catch a tram.

"Most major cities around the world already let you tap on with the card in your wallet," she said. "This brings Melbourne into line and removes one more barrier to using public transport."

Fares will not change, and the system will apply the same daily and weekly caps that currently apply to myki users. Concession card holders will still need to register a myki or a linked account to receive discounted fares.

The upgrade is part of a $1.6 billion contract signed in 2023 to replace the ageing ticketing system, which has been criticised for slow card readers and frequent faults.

Daniel Bowen, president of the Public Transport Users Association, said the change was long overdue but warned that the rollout needed to be reliable. "Passengers have been promised improvements to the ticketing system for years," he said. "The government needs to make sure this works properly on day one."

A record number of loggerhead turtle nests have been recorded on Queensland's Mon Repos beach this season, giving conservationists hope that decades of protection are starting to pay off.

Rangers counted 512 loggerhead nests between November and January, the highest number since monitoring began in 1968. The beach, near Bundaberg, is the most important nesting site for loggerhead turtles in the South Pacific.

Loggerhead numbers fell sharply in the 1970s and 1980s, largely because turtles were drowning in trawling nets. The introduction of turtle exclusion devices on fishing boats in the early 2000s, along with fox control and restrictions on beachfront lighting, has helped the population slowly recover.

Senior ranger Cathy Limpus said the result was encouraging but should be treated with caution.

"Loggerheads don't nest every year, so numbers can jump around a lot from season to season," she said. "What matters is the long-term trend, and that's been gradually improving."

Rising temperatures remain a concern. The sex of a turtle hatchling is determined by the temperature of the sand, and warmer nests produce more females. Researchers have found that some recent seasons have produced almost entirely female hatchlings at warmer beaches.

Rangers have trialled shading some nests and relocating eggs to cooler parts of the beach. The Mon Repos turtle centre, which runs guided night tours during the nesting season, will reopen to visitors in November.

Explain the significance of the Eureka Stockade in Australian history.

The Eureka Stockade of December 1854 was a short and unsuccessful armed rebellion, but its significance in Australian history is far greater than the battle itself. It exposed the injustice of the colonial licensing system, led directly to political reforms in Victoria, and later became an important symbol of democracy and resistance to authority.

The rebellion grew out of the miners' anger at the gold licence. Every miner had to pay 30 shillings a month whether or not they found any gold, and police carried out frequent, often violent "licence hunts". Miners also had no right to vote or own land. When a hotel owner accused of murdering a miner was cleared by a local magistrate in October 1854, tensions in Ballarat boiled over. Miners formed the Ballarat Reform League, burned their licences and built a rough stockade under the Southern Cross flag.

In the early hours of 3 December, government troops and police attacked. The fighting lasted about fifteen minutes. Around 22 miners and six soldiers were killed.

Although the miners were defeated, public opinion turned against the government. Thirteen rebels were tried for high treason, and Melbourne juries acquitted every one. A royal commission criticised the goldfields administration, and within a year the licence was replaced by a cheaper miner's right, which included the right to vote. Peter Lalor, the rebel leader, was later elected to parliament.

Over time, Eureka took on wider meaning. The Southern Cross flag has been adopted by groups ranging from trade unions to nationalist movements, and the event is often described as the birthplace of Australian democracy. Historians debate how much credit Eureka deserves for reforms that were already being discussed, but its symbolic importance is clear.

Is social media doing more harm than good for young people? Discuss.

Social media is now a central part of young people's lives, and debates about its effects tend to swing between panic and dismissal. The evidence suggests a more complicated picture: social media brings real benefits, but for some young people, particularly heavy users, the harms are significant and deserve attention.

The benefits are easy to overlook. Social media helps young people maintain friendships, especially across distance, and can offer support to those who feel isolated in their local communities. Teenagers who are LGBTQ+, have a disability or have a rare interest often find others like them online. Platforms also give young people a place to create and share work, from music and art to short films.

However, the risks are real. Studies have found links between heavy social media use and higher rates of anxiety, depression and poor sleep, especially among teenage girls. Constant exposure to edited images can damage body image, and cyberbullying can follow a young person home in a way that schoolyard bullying could not. Many platforms are designed to maximise time spent on them, using infinite scrolling and notifications that make it hard to stop.

It is important to note that most of this research shows correlation rather than causation. Young people who are already struggling may use social media more, rather than social media causing their problems. The effects also seem to depend heavily on how it is used: passive scrolling appears more harmful than actively messaging friends.

Overall, social media is neither wholly harmful nor harmless. The most useful response is not a blanket ban but better design, sensible limits and teaching young people to notice how it affects them.

What is a mortgage offset account and is it worth having?

An offset account is a transaction account linked to your home loan. The balance in it is "offset" against the amount you owe, so you only pay interest on the difference.

For example, if your loan is $500,000 and you have $40,000 in your offset account, you're charged interest as if you only owed $460,000. Your money is still available to spend at any time, but while it sits there it's effectively earning the same rate as your mortgage interest, and you don't pay tax on that saving the way you would on interest from a normal savings account.

When it's worth it:
- You keep a reasonable amount of cash in the bank, such as an emergency fund or savings for a future expense.
- Your salary goes straight into the account, so the balance builds up between pay days.
- Your mortgage rate is higher than what you'd earn in a high-interest savings account after tax.

When it might not be:
- Loans with offset accounts often have higher interest rates or annual fees, sometimes $300 to $400 a year. If you only keep a few thousand dollars in it, the saving may not cover the cost.
- If you'd rather lock your savings away so you're not tempted to spend them, making extra repayments might suit you better.

A simple test is to multiply your typical offset balance by your interest rate. If that's comfortably more than the extra fees, the offset account is probably worth it.

It's worth getting advice for your situation, but for many homeowners an offset account is one of the easiest ways to pay off a loan faster.

Year 10 Science excursion reflection

On our excursion to the Queensland Museum, we visited the Wild State gallery and the dinosaur exhibition, and took part in a workshop about how scientists classify animals.

The most interesting part of the day for me was the workshop. We were given a collection of insect specimens and had to sort them into groups using a dichotomous key. At first our group kept getting stuck because we were looking at colour, which turned out to be a bad way to classify insects. The museum educator explained that features like the number of wings, the type of mouthparts and the structure of the legs are more reliable, because colour can vary a lot within the same species. Once we started looking at those features, it became much easier.

In the dinosaur exhibition, I learned that many scientists now classify birds as living dinosaurs, because they share features like hollow bones and feathers with theropods such as Velociraptor. I had heard this before, but seeing the skeletons side by side made it much more convincing.

One thing I would change about the excursion is the amount of time we had in each gallery. We had to rush through Wild State, and I didn't get to read most of the information about Queensland's endangered species, which was the part most connected to our current unit.

Overall, the excursion helped me understand that classification is based on evidence and can change when new evidence is found, which links directly to what we have been learning about evolution in class.
