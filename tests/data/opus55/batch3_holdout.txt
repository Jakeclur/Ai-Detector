The first snow in forty years fell on the town of Stanthorpe on a Tuesday, and Lucy's grandfather cried.

She didn't understand it at first. She was nine, and to her the snow was pure magic: soft and silent and strange, settling on the fence posts and the wheelbarrow and the backs of the sheep in the paddock. She ran outside in her pyjamas and gumboots and stood with her face turned up, catching flakes on her tongue.

When she came back in, her grandfather was standing at the kitchen window, holding a cup of tea he wasn't drinking. His eyes were wet.

"Pop? Are you okay?"

"I'm fine, love." He wiped his face with the back of his hand. "The last time it snowed like this, your nan and I had just bought this place. We built a snowman right there by the shed. She gave it my hat." He laughed, a small cracked sound. "I never got it back."

Lucy looked at the shed, already turning white. Then she went to the hallway, found his old felt hat on the hook, and pulled on her coat.

It took them most of the morning. Her grandfather rolled the bottom, slowly, stopping to rest every few minutes. Lucy did the middle and the head. They used two pinecones for eyes and a carrot from the vegetable drawer for a nose.

Then Lucy stood on the upturned wheelbarrow and placed the hat on top.

Her grandfather stood back and looked at it for a long time.

"Well," he said finally, his voice thick. "I suppose she'd want it to have one."

How does Shakespeare present the relationship between Romeo and Juliet as doomed from the start?

From the opening prologue to the final scene, Shakespeare presents Romeo and Juliet's love as doomed. Through the chorus, repeated imagery of death and the speed of the relationship, he makes the audience aware of the tragic ending long before the characters are.

The prologue tells the audience the outcome before the play has even begun. The lovers are described as "star-crossed", suggesting that fate is against them, and their love is called "death-marked". By revealing that their deaths will end the feud, Shakespeare creates dramatic irony: the audience watches every hopeful moment knowing how it will end.

Imagery of death appears throughout the play, even in the most romantic scenes. When Juliet first learns who Romeo is, she says, "My grave is like to be my wedding bed." Later, Romeo says he would rather die than live without her. These lines show that, for the lovers, love and death are closely linked from the beginning.

The speed of the relationship also suggests danger. Romeo and Juliet meet, fall in love and marry within about a day. Friar Lawrence warns that "these violent delights have violent ends", suggesting that such intense, rushed love cannot last. His words foreshadow how quickly everything will fall apart.

Finally, the feud between the Montagues and Capulets creates a world where the lovers have no safe place. Every decision they make must be secret, and each secret makes the situation more fragile.

Through these techniques, Shakespeare ensures the audience feels the weight of fate throughout the play.

Is homework beneficial for primary school students? Discuss.

Homework has been part of primary school for generations, but in recent years more parents, teachers and researchers have questioned whether it actually helps younger children learn. The evidence suggests that small amounts of the right kind of homework can be useful, but large amounts in the early years offer little benefit and may cause harm.

Supporters of homework argue that it helps children practise skills learned in class, such as reading and times tables. Regular practice can make these skills automatic, which frees up children's thinking for harder tasks. Homework can also help children develop habits like organisation and responsibility, and it gives parents a window into what their child is learning.

However, research suggests that the academic benefits of homework for primary students are small. Studies by researchers such as Harris Cooper have found a strong link between homework and achievement in secondary school, but only a weak link in primary school. For younger children, time spent playing, reading for pleasure, being active and spending time with family may be more valuable.

Homework can also create stress and conflict at home, especially when tasks are too difficult or unclear. It may disadvantage children whose parents work long hours, speak a different language or lack resources to help.

On balance, the most useful approach for primary students is likely to be light, purposeful homework, such as daily reading, rather than worksheets that add pressure without clear benefit. Schools should consider family circumstances and focus on quality rather than quantity.

How do vaccines create herd immunity?

Herd immunity happens when enough people in a community are immune to a disease that it can't spread easily, which protects people who aren't immune.

Infectious diseases spread from person to person. If someone with measles walks into a room full of people with no immunity, they might infect a dozen others, each of whom infects a dozen more. The disease spreads quickly through the population.

But if most people in that room are immune, because they've been vaccinated or have had the disease before, the virus has nowhere to go. The infected person might pass it to one or two people at most, and the chain of infection quickly breaks.

This matters because some people can't be vaccinated or don't respond well to vaccines. That includes newborn babies who are too young for certain vaccines, people with weakened immune systems such as those having chemotherapy, and people with certain allergies. Herd immunity protects them indirectly.

The percentage of people who need to be immune depends on how contagious the disease is. For measles, which is extremely contagious, it's about 95 per cent. For polio, it's around 80 per cent.

When vaccination rates fall below these levels, outbreaks can happen even in countries where a disease had almost disappeared. That's what happened with measles in parts of Europe and the United States in the late 2010s.

So when you get vaccinated, you're not only protecting yourself, but also the people around you who can't protect themselves.

Why is the ocean salty?

The ocean is salty mostly because of rocks on land.

When rain falls, it picks up a small amount of carbon dioxide from the air, which makes it very slightly acidic. As this rainwater runs over rocks and soil, it slowly dissolves minerals, including sodium, calcium and potassium. Rivers carry these dissolved minerals to the sea.

River water still tastes fresh because the amount of salt in it is tiny. But once the water reaches the ocean, the minerals stay behind. When seawater evaporates to form clouds, only the water rises; the salt is left in the sea. Over hundreds of millions of years, this has built up the ocean's saltiness to about 3.5 per cent, or roughly 35 grams of salt in every litre.

There's a second source too. Hydrothermal vents on the sea floor release hot, mineral-rich water from inside the Earth's crust, adding more dissolved minerals. Undersea volcanoes contribute as well.

The most common salt in seawater is sodium chloride, the same as table salt, but there are many others, including magnesium and calcium compounds.

The ocean isn't getting noticeably saltier today, though. Salt is also removed over time as it forms new rocks on the sea floor and is absorbed by marine life, so the inputs and outputs have roughly balanced out.

Saltiness does vary from place to place. Seas in hot, dry regions with lots of evaporation, like the Red Sea, are saltier, while areas where large rivers flow in or ice melts are less salty.

What causes thunderstorms?

Thunderstorms form when warm, moist air rises quickly into cooler air higher in the atmosphere. Three ingredients are needed: moisture, unstable air and something to lift the air upwards.

On a hot day, the sun heats the ground, which warms the air just above it. Warm air is lighter than cool air, so it starts to rise. As it rises, it cools, and the water vapour in it condenses into tiny droplets, forming a cloud. Condensation releases heat, which makes the air inside the cloud even warmer than its surroundings, so it keeps rising faster.

If the air is unstable enough, this upward motion can carry the cloud up 10 to 15 kilometres, forming a towering cumulonimbus cloud with a flat, anvil-shaped top.

Inside the cloud, water droplets and ice crystals are thrown around by strong updrafts and downdrafts. As they collide, they build up electrical charges, with negative charges usually gathering near the bottom of the cloud and positive charges near the top. When the difference becomes large enough, electricity discharges as lightning.

Lightning heats the air around it to about 30,000 degrees Celsius in a fraction of a second. The air expands so suddenly that it creates a shock wave, which we hear as thunder.

Eventually, rain and hail become too heavy for the updrafts to hold. They fall, dragging cooler air down with them, and the storm begins to weaken.

In Queensland, thunderstorms are most common in late spring and summer, when the air is hot and humid.

Book review: Holes by Louis Sachar

Holes follows Stanley Yelnats, a boy who is sent to a juvenile detention camp in the Texas desert for a crime he didn't commit. At Camp Green Lake, which has no lake and nothing green, the boys spend every day digging holes five feet wide and five feet deep, supposedly to "build character".

What makes the book clever is the way Sachar weaves three stories together. Alongside Stanley's time at the camp, we learn about his great-great-grandfather and a family curse, and about an outlaw named Kissin' Kate Barlow who lived near the lake a hundred years earlier. At first these stories seem unrelated, but by the end every detail connects, and the payoff is very satisfying.

Stanley is an easy character to like. He starts the book unlucky and lonely, but he slowly becomes braver and more confident, especially through his friendship with Zero, a quiet boy everyone underestimates.

The book also deals with serious ideas, including injustice, racism and the way people can be trapped by their circumstances, but it never feels heavy. The writing is simple and often funny, and the short chapters keep the story moving.

If I had one criticism, it's that the ending ties everything up a little too neatly. Real life rarely works out that perfectly.

Overall, Holes is a brilliant, original novel that works for younger and older readers alike.

Rating: 4.5 out of 5
