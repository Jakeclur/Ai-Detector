Inflation is when prices across the economy rise over time, so each dollar buys a bit less than it used to. A little inflation, around 2 to 3% a year, is normal and is what the Reserve Bank aims for. Problems start when it runs much higher.

There are three main causes.

Demand-pull inflation. When people and businesses want to buy more than the economy can produce, sellers can raise prices. This often happens when interest rates are low, wages are rising, or the government is spending heavily.

Cost-push inflation. When it gets more expensive to make things, businesses pass those costs on. A jump in oil prices is the classic example, because fuel feeds into transport, farming and manufacturing. Supply problems, like the shipping delays after COVID, work the same way.

Expectations. If everyone expects prices to keep rising, workers ask for bigger pay rises and businesses raise prices in advance. That can keep inflation going even after the original cause fades.

Central banks fight inflation mainly by raising interest rates. Higher rates make borrowing more expensive and saving more attractive, so people spend less and demand cools. The trade-off is that it can slow the economy and push up unemployment, which is why rate decisions get so much attention.

Weather is what's happening in the atmosphere right now or over the next few days. Climate is the average pattern of weather in a place over a long period, usually 30 years or more.

A simple way to remember it: weather is your mood, climate is your personality.

If it's 35 degrees and humid in Brisbane today, that's weather. The fact that Brisbane has hot, wet summers and mild, dry winters is its climate.

This difference matters when people talk about climate change. A single cold week, or even an unusually cold year, doesn't disprove global warming, because that's weather. Climate change is about the long-term trend. Australia's average temperature has risen by about 1.5 degrees since 1910, and heatwaves have become more frequent and more intense. You can only see that by looking at decades of data, not at any one day.

Scientists use different tools for each. Weather forecasts rely on current measurements and computer models that look a few days ahead. Climate studies look at long records, ice cores, tree rings and models that project how averages might shift over decades.

Every morning, thousands of Australian students spend time deciding what to wear, worrying about whether their clothes are the right brand, and comparing themselves to classmates. School uniforms remove that pressure, and that is why every school should keep them.

The strongest argument for uniforms is fairness. When everyone wears the same thing, it is much harder to tell which students come from wealthy families and which do not. Students who cannot afford expensive clothes are less likely to be teased or left out, and the focus shifts from what people own to who they are.

Uniforms also save families money. A few sets of uniform last a whole year, while keeping up with fashion can cost far more. Many schools also run second-hand uniform shops, which makes the cost even lower.

Another benefit is a sense of belonging. Wearing the school uniform at sport carnivals, excursions or community events shows that students are part of something bigger. It builds pride and makes students easy to identify and keep safe on trips.

Critics argue that uniforms limit self-expression. This is a fair point, but students still have plenty of ways to express themselves, through their ideas, their work, their hobbies and what they wear on weekends. School is a place for learning, and a uniform helps keep it that way.

For these reasons, school uniforms do far more good than harm and should stay.

Social media has become a normal part of teenage life. Most Australian teenagers use at least one platform every day, and many check their phones dozens of times. Its effects are mixed, and they depend a lot on how it is used.

On the positive side, social media helps teenagers stay connected with friends, especially those who have moved away or go to different schools. It can give teenagers who feel isolated, such as LGBTQ+ young people or those with rare interests, a community where they feel accepted. It is also a place to be creative, through videos, art and music, and to learn about issues like climate change.

However, there are real downsides. Constant comparison with filtered photos can harm self-esteem and body image, particularly for girls. Cyberbullying can follow students home in a way that schoolyard bullying never could. Many apps are designed to keep users scrolling, which cuts into sleep, homework and face-to-face time. Research has linked heavy use, especially late at night, with higher rates of anxiety and depression, although scientists still debate how much social media causes these problems and how much it reflects them.

Recent laws, such as Australia's minimum age for some platforms, show that governments are taking these risks seriously. Still, bans alone are unlikely to solve the problem. Teaching teenagers to recognise unhealthy patterns, set limits and think critically about what they see is just as important.

The building manager stared at it for a long time. Unit 7 waited for the order to paint over it.

Instead, she took a photo, and then she went and bought more colours.

Nuclear power is one of the most debated energy options, and there are strong arguments on both sides.

Pros:
- Low emissions. Once a plant is running, it produces almost no greenhouse gases, making it one of the cleanest sources of electricity over its lifetime.
- Reliable output. Unlike solar and wind, nuclear plants run around the clock regardless of the weather, which helps keep the grid stable.
- Small footprint. A nuclear plant produces a huge amount of power from a relatively small area of land.
- Long lifespan. Modern plants can operate for 60 years or more.

Cons:
- Cost and time. New plants are extremely expensive and often take 10 to 20 years to build, with frequent delays and budget blowouts.
- Waste. Spent fuel stays radioactive for thousands of years, and no country has yet opened a permanent deep storage site at scale.
- Accident risk. Serious accidents are rare, but events like Chernobyl and Fukushima show the consequences can be severe and long-lasting.
- Not yet legal in Australia. Nuclear power is currently banned under federal law, so the country would need new laws, regulators and a trained workforce before building anything.

Whether nuclear makes sense depends on the country. For places with little space for renewables, it can be a strong option. In Australia, the debate mostly comes down to cost and timing compared with building more renewables and storage.

A black hole is a place in space where gravity is so strong that nothing can escape, not even light.

They form when a very big star runs out of fuel. Without the energy from nuclear reactions pushing outwards, the star's core collapses under its own weight. If there's enough mass, it keeps collapsing into an incredibly small, dense point.

The edge of a black hole is called the event horizon. Outside it, you could still escape if you went fast enough. Once you cross it, escape is impossible, because you'd need to travel faster than light. That's why black holes look black: no light from inside can reach us.

We can't see black holes directly, but we can see their effects. Stars orbit around them at very high speeds, and gas falling towards them heats up and glows brightly before it crosses the event horizon. In 2019, astronomers released the first image of a black hole's shadow, surrounded by a ring of glowing gas.

Black holes come in different sizes. Stellar black holes are a few to dozens of times the mass of the Sun. Supermassive black holes, millions or billions of times the Sun's mass, sit at the centres of most galaxies, including our own Milky Way.

And no, Earth isn't going to be sucked into one. The nearest known black hole is about 1,500 light-years away.

In "Dulce et Decorum Est," Wilfred Owen uses vivid imagery and a shocking shift in pace to destroy the idea that dying for your country is noble.

The poem opens with soldiers who are nothing like the heroes of patriotic posters. They are "bent double, like old beggars under sacks," "knock-kneed, coughing like hags." These similes compare young men to old, broken figures, showing how the war has drained them. Owen's slow, heavy rhythm in the first stanza mirrors their exhausted march.

The second stanza breaks this rhythm with a sudden cry: "Gas! GAS! Quick, boys!" The exclamation marks and short phrases create panic. One soldier fails to fit his mask in time, and Owen describes him "flound'ring like a man in fire or lime." The image is drawn out and horrifying, and in the next stanza it returns in a dream, "guttering, choking, drowning," showing how the memory haunts the speaker.

In the final stanza, Owen turns directly to the reader, and probably to Jessie Pope, a writer of pro-war poems. He describes the dying man's "froth-corrupted lungs" in grotesque detail, then tells the reader that if they had seen this, they would not repeat "the old Lie: Dulce et decorum est / Pro patria mori." By calling the Latin motto a lie, Owen makes the poem's purpose clear: to expose the gap between how war is sold and how it is experienced.

There wasn't one single reason the Western Roman Empire fell. It was a long decline driven by several problems that fed into each other, and historians still argue about which mattered most.

Political instability. In the third century especially, emperors were overthrown and murdered with alarming regularity. Over about 50 years there were more than 20 emperors. Constant civil wars drained money and soldiers and made long-term planning almost impossible.

Economic trouble. Heavy taxes, inflation caused by debasing the coinage, and disrupted trade weakened the economy. Many farmers abandoned their land, and cities shrank.

Military pressure. Germanic groups such as the Goths, Vandals and Franks pressed against the borders, partly because they were being pushed west by the Huns. Rome increasingly relied on these same groups as soldiers, and their loyalty was often uncertain.

A divided empire. In 395 the empire was permanently split into eastern and western halves. The East, centred on Constantinople, was richer and easier to defend. The West was left with weaker resources and longer borders.

Key events. The Visigoths sacked Rome in 410, and the Vandals did the same in 455. The traditional end date is 476, when the Germanic leader Odoacer removed the last western emperor, Romulus Augustulus.

The eastern half, known today as the Byzantine Empire, survived for nearly another thousand years, until 1453.
